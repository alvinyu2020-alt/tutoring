Approach

From the first lesson, I try to always establish what the goals of our sessions are (exam, coursework, etc). I try to lay out as clearly as possible what the process of achieving that goal looks like.

I try to build an atmosphere of communal activity, treating all children as equals. I generally use "we" when speaking, because I see the tutor and the child as a team, with a common, shared goal.

The philosophy of my lessons is skills-based and practice-led. I believe that all academic skills — whether it be arithmetic, essay-writing, or creative writing - can be learned through deliberate practice, much like learning to play an instrument or a sport.

Rather than speaking at a student passively, I want them to be doing. The core of this teaching style is therefore discrete exercises - simple, chunked, fun puzzles that help the practical application of the subject. (More examples of this can be found in the approach section.)

I also encourage web-based learning, for example using gamification to memorise large chunks of information and developing quizzes to aid vocabulary learning.

Ultimately, the end goal will be a student who can think, write, and solve academic problems independently, rather than one who has simply memorised the right essay or formula.

Sometimes this approach has unexpected results. One of my students inadvertently used my creative writing techniques to write a novel, which went on to receive a publishing offer!

Exams, of course, are a different beast. Closer to an exam, I will start using past papers and work on timed practice, to slowly bring skills learned over the year into practice. I'll work on localised areas of weakness — timing, nerves, essay structure - and iron these out one by one.

Despite this rigour of preparation, I have a very relaxed, calming attitude during class. There is a large body of scientific research indicating that improving at any skill requires two things: (a) focused, discrete improvement on targeted weaknesses and (b) honest, clear feedback from an external source. My tutoring is informed by these two principles — and through hard work I genuinely believe I can teach children the skills to succeed not just in their exams but in later life.

Approach

The core of my approach is targeted and specific. I work to identify core weaknesses and strengths in a student's skillset, ensuring that I praise the strengths of the student, and find practical solutions to work on their weaknesses. My focus with tutoring is to fill in the gaps that school teachers generally leave behind - in a hands-on and practical way.

One important part of my approach is that I build skills, alongside content. I don't believe speaking at children for an hour is very helpful - the goal of my lessons is for my students to be 'active' as opposed to 'passive'.

I teach these skills throughout our lessons through a series of exercises that encourage independence of thought. Discrete exercises are the core of my teaching practice - simple, chunked, fun puzzles, exercises that help practical application of the subject.

I also encourage web and internet based learning, for example using gamification to memorise large chunks of information, and developing quizzes to aid vocabulary learning.

English

In English, skills would involve:

  • Basic analytical skills: how to explain the effectiveness of a metaphor
  • Vocabulary and prose
  • Structure
  • Memorisation
  • Exam technique

On the writing side, I help students fine-tune their prose through writing exercises. This might be improving their technical vocabulary and removing colloquialisms, helping their sentence-by-sentence construction, or for the more advanced students, helping them understand argument threading and signposting.

On the analytical side, this involves laterally examining something like a metaphor, and being able to offer or explain its effectiveness. For example, we might spend ten minutes of every lesson looking at objects like 'a can of coke' and writing down as many things Coca-Cola could symbolise, to help their analytical, creative skills. We would work towards making these exercises practical as soon as possible by weaving this generative lateral thinking into essay writing.

Maths

My approach in Maths is fairly straightforward. I will find 5 core areas of weakness - let's say angles, geometry, fractions, algebra and ratio. I will sit down each week and unpack one area of weakness, doing targeted exercises from the ground up. I will use a variety of different methods to try and explain a topic - using bean counters, or a deck of cards, and try to apply this as much as possible to real-life problems. Once I have taught a topic confidently, my priority is the retention of information.

I will consistently thread in the previous subject, to ensure that knowledge is sufficiently ingrained. I am a firm believer in spaced repetition and content threading. Spaced repetition is an evidence-based learning technique where new and more difficult material is shown more frequently, while older and less difficult material is shown less frequently, in order to exploit the psychological spacing effect.

Example Lessons

To give you a sense of what I actually teach, here are two short examples of the kind of thing that might come up in a lesson.

Specificity vs description

Creative writing teaching is commonly mis-taught. Although it's an integral part of the GCSE English Language syllabus (25%!), and of most 11+ English exams, often it leads to a lot of scratched heads.

Very often students write and struggle with a creative writing piece. And the teacher's feedback inevitably reads the same— be more descriptive! Use more adjectives! Too many spelling mistakes!

And the student goes away and writes a new creative writing piece, but receive the same feedback, doomed never to actually improve their grade.

I want to give a 5 minute lesson that will change the way you think of creative writing.

Specificity vs description

'Be more descriptive' — probably the most oft-heard feedback in creative writing. And it really cringes me out because it isn't wrong — it's often poorly understood.

What we need to do is to get at the heart of what 'descriptive' means. Because more description doesn't mean better description.

Take the sentence:

The sun shines on the flowers.

I could be more descriptive here. I could add more adjectives (which is usually the port-of-call of most students).

So then we get something like this:

The big, beautiful sun shines on the blue lake and lovely flowers.

The trap is — the above sentence is not really better. The words 'big', 'beautiful' don't really add much to the sentence. So more adjective doesn't mean better writing.

So what is the answer?

It's specificity of description.

Good creative writing is about being minutely specific.

Good writers know the names of every plant: chrysanthemum, rhododendrons, sunflowers. Colours that you can paint with: cerulean blue, mustard yellow, amber red. Good writers can tell you what specific toothpaste brand their character's use and the feeling of the glycerin mixed with artificial mint.

Good writers don't just say 'dog'. They name the specific breed: a cocker spaniel, a labrador, or a beagle. Because reading is about visualising, and how can you visualise a big, sad dog? But you can visualise a golden retriever instantly, in far fewer words.

Good writers don't use unspecific verbs — walks, runs, they use metaphors in their verbs - canter, sprint, danced, beamed.

And if we apply the above technique to the 'boring' sentence…

The clay-red sun beamed over the shimmering, azure lake, and the violet pink chrysanthemums flowers.

I've not really changed the subject, or syntax of this sentence. But I've added in specificity. Sure, it's not perfect — but you can see it.

So that's my first lesson for creative writing. Don't be more descriptive. Be more specific.

How to read a word problem

The most common complaint I hear from parents is some version of this: they can do Maths, but they really struggle with worded problems.

The answer, almost always, is not that the student doesn't understand the Maths. It is that they haven't been taught how to read a Maths question.

Most students read a word problem in the same way they would read a story. They start at the beginning, read through to the end, and hope that the answer will somehow reveal itself. They are trying to read, understand and calculate all at the same time, which can make even a relatively simple question seem much more complicated than it actually is.

Take the following question:

A bat and a ball cost £1.10 in total. The bat costs £1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?

Most students will instinctively say 10p.

This is a really interesting mistake because the student usually does understand the individual pieces of information. They know that £1.10 minus £1 is 10p. The problem is that they have substituted the difficult problem for a simpler one. The phrase "the bat costs £1 more than the ball" has effectively become "the bat costs £1". The brain has simplified the question without the student realising it.

But if the ball costs 10p, and the bat costs £1 more than the ball, then the bat must cost £1.10. That would make the total £1.20, not £1.10.

So how do we approach the problem differently?

Step 1: Read to the end first and find the question. Before picking up a pencil, I want the student to work out what they are actually being asked to find. Here, the question is asking us to find the price of the ball. We know that the bat and the ball together cost £1.10, and we know that the bat costs £1 more than the ball. So rather than trying to solve the whole problem in our heads, we can start by turning those pieces of information into Maths:

Bat + ball = £1.10
Bat = ball + £1

If we check our (incorrect) answer of 10p for the ball, this would not work with both statements. If the ball is 10p, then the bat would be £1. But then the bat and the ball together would be £1.20!

Step 2: Turn the words into Maths. This is the part that students often haven't been explicitly taught. A word problem is essentially a Maths problem hidden inside a paragraph of English. Our job is to find the Maths. In this case, we have an unknown quantity, so let's call the ball x. If ball = x, then the bat must be x + 1, because the bat costs £1 more than the ball. We can now put it into an equation:

(x + 1) + x = 1.10

At this point, the word problem has disappeared. We are no longer trying to understand a story about a bat and a ball. We have a simple algebra problem in front of us.

Step 3: Solve it.

2x + 1 = 1.10
2x = 0.10
x = £0.05

So the ball costs 5p. We can then check the answer. If the ball costs 5p, the bat must cost £1.05. Together, they cost £1.10, so we know our answer is correct.

The important thing here isn't really the algebra. It is the process of getting from the words to the algebra in the first place. A lot of students who tell me they "can't do word problems" can actually do the Maths perfectly well. What they struggle with is knowing which information matters, what the question is asking them to find, and how to translate the language of the question into something mathematical. Once you teach them how to read the question in this way, word problems become much less intimidating.
